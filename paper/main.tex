\title{Can Agents Trust a Replication Verifier?\\
Epistemic Contracts for Scientific Agent Tools}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Luxin Zhang}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Independent Researcher\\ luxin.zhang92@outlook.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
AI agents that do science increasingly hand the question ``can this finding be trusted?'' to a verifier tool. Such tools are judged on accuracy, not on the guarantees the calling agent silently depends on. We propose a testable methodology for \emph{epistemic contracts}: explicit guarantees about what a tool's output may reflect (temporal validity, isolation from the queried paper's labels), which decisions it supports, and how it is audited. We apply it to \textsc{SciReliGraph}, an MCP replication verifier whose contract tests reach a verified mutation score of \mutVoneScore{} (\mutVthreeScore{} after tests for surviving mutants). On 274 SCORE claims with replication outcomes, a verifier meeting the integrity requirements discriminates only modestly (AUROC \aucBone{}), and citation graphs bring no established improvement. The best LLM judges rank claims competitively, but their temporal validity cannot be certified, and a decoy probe caught two of four judges naming outcomes for never-replicated papers. In an agent-in-the-loop experiment with no-tool and placebo controls, a bare probability pushed most agents into near-chance automatic decisions they avoided without the tool. The contract removed most of these errors for three of four agents, beyond a generic warning; the fourth misread it. In a pre-specified follow-up, wordings that state the permitted action fixed this for all four agents, and with a synthetic decision-ready verifier the same contract let agents automate exactly the licensed claims (4.7\% wrong vs.\ 12--15\% with a bare number). Contracts can tell agents when automation is warranted, but must be tested against the agents that read them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Systems that automate parts of research~\cite{lu2024} keep having to decide which earlier findings are solid enough to build on. They can hand that decision to a \emph{verifier} tool, for instance an LLM acting as a judge~\cite{zheng2023}. The stakes are real. Large replication projects found that many published findings in the social and behavioural sciences do not hold up~\cite{osc2015,camerer2016,camerer2018}, and the DARPA SCORE program has released outcomes for 274 replicated claims from 164 papers, of which 151 (55.1\%) replicated~\cite{tyner2026,score_g5sny}. There are also reasons for caution about delegating. LLMs that predict experimental outcomes cannot tell which of their predictions are reliable~\cite{sehwag2026scipredict}, and AI-led teams reproduced far fewer published results than human-only or AI-assisted teams~\cite{brodeur2026aiteams}.

When an agent asks a verifier about claim $c$ at time $t$, it quietly assumes three things: that the answer uses only information available at $t$, that the probability means what it says, and that the answer can be audited later. We call these guarantees an \emph{epistemic contract}. Security and correctness checks ask whether a tool is safe and behaves as specified; an epistemic contract states what the tool's \emph{output may reflect} and which decisions it \emph{licenses}. None of the ingredients is new on its own. Design by contract~\cite{meyer1992}, model documentation~\cite{mitchell2019}, calibration~\cite{guo2017}, metamorphic~\cite{chen2018mt} and mutation testing~\cite{jia2011}, and tool-use evaluation~\cite{wu2026mcpmark} all have long histories. Our contribution is a \emph{testable methodology} that combines them for agent tools, together with evidence of a failure mode that only shows up when they are combined. These guarantees also fail silently. A verifier fitted on bibliometric data scraped today can absorb signals from after the replication, an LLM judge may simply remember the outcome~\cite{sainz2023}, and random splits can put claims from the same paper in both training and test data~\cite{kaufman2012,kapoor2023}.

Our question is simple: \emph{if a verifier is built and tested against an explicit epistemic contract, is it good enough for an agent to rely on, and does the agent use it correctly?} We expected time-bounded citation graphs (H1), corrected for non-independent support (H2), to improve forecasts, and lifting the evidence cutoff to inflate apparent accuracy (H3). The data supported none of the three, and we report these negative results in full. The agent experiment, by contrast, changed our view of what a contract must cover. One agent read the tool's honest warning that its score was weak as evidence \emph{against the claim} and rejected \agHaikuACRej{} of the claims it saw (\S\ref{sec:agent}). A contract therefore has to be tested against its \emph{consumers}, not only against the tool.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A testable epistemic-contract methodology for verifier tools} (\S\ref{sec:contract}): five requirements, a typing of features by when they become available, and a reusable JSON Schema with tool-agnostic test relations. The typing caught a leak in our own pipeline that would otherwise have produced our best-looking result.
\item \emph{Contract tests with verified adequacy} (\S\ref{sec:harness}--\S\ref{sec:tool}): an MCP tool that enforces the contract on every call, metamorphic and isolation tests, and mutation analysis with both targeted mutants and an independently re-checked standard tool.
\item \emph{A decoy-calibrated memorization probe for LLM judges} (\S\ref{sec:llm}). Asking judges about journal-matched papers that were never replicated or reproduced separates genuine recall from fabrication; two of the four models named outcomes for such papers.
\item \emph{Evidence of a gap between contract and consumer} (\S\ref{sec:agent}). A bare verifier probability induced errors that most agents avoided without the tool; the contract removed most of them for three agents but misled the fourth. A pre-specified follow-up shows that stating the permitted action fixes this for all four, and lets agents automate when a tool is good enough to act on.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Background and Related Work}
\textbf{Replication prediction.} Prediction markets~\cite{dreber2015} classified 103 pooled replication outcomes with 73\% accuracy~\cite{gordon2021}, and for SCORE, human forecasters reached a median AUC of 0.76 on 37 high-powered replications~\cite{mody2026}. Machine-learning forecasters built on statistical and textual features also report above-chance accuracy~\cite{altmejd2019,yang2020,youyou2023}. SCORE's own machine assessments~\cite{alipourfard2021} were mostly built to predict \emph{human} judgements and did not discriminate replication outcomes~\cite{rajtmajer2026}. Other work adds algorithmic traders to prediction markets~\cite{chakravorti2026hybrid}, benchmarks agents that \emph{carry out} replications~\cite{nguyen2026replicatorbench}, or uses LLMs to screen reproducibility, though not to replace expert reanalysis~\cite{holtdirk2026reproducibility}. We differ in three ways: we use only realised outcomes as labels, we enforce temporal validity, and we evaluate the verifier as a tool that an agent acts on. Nonreplicable findings are known to be cited more~\cite{serragarcia2021}; we ask whether citation \emph{structure} helps where raw counts do not. Unlike fact-checking~\cite{wadden2020}, replication forecasting predicts a future study, which exposes it to hindsight leakage.

\textbf{LLM judgements and agent tools.} Evaluations of LLM forecasters are vulnerable to temporal leakage~\cite{paleka2026pitfalls}. GLEAN turns trajectory evidence into calibrated correctness probabilities for high-stakes agent verification~\cite{zhang2026glean}, and claim-level calibration makes LLM confidences usable for decisions, with post-hoc temperature and Platt scaling as baselines~\cite{abbasli2026claimcal}. Work on MCP evaluates multi-step tool use~\cite{wu2026mcpmark}, explores tool spaces with metamorphic checks~\cite{liu2026sgvef}, defends the tool layer at run time~\cite{yergattikar2026securing}, and records agents' actions during experiment reproduction~\cite{hong2026agentactionbench}. These studies ask whether agents \emph{use} tools correctly or safely. Other work shows how agents mishandle what tools return: they adopt corrupted returns at high rates, and reliability labels barely change this~\cite{yang2026overreliance}. Uncertainty gets lost at handoffs unless it is carried explicitly~\cite{shi2026laundering}, and passing through a tool boundary does not by itself give a statement epistemic warrant~\cite{romanchuk2026semantic}. Agents also struggle to know when not to act~\cite{liu2026agentabstain} and how much time has passed~\cite{cheng2026temporally}. Existing tool contracts constrain calls and actions, through preconditions and effects~\cite{sureshbabu2026contract2tool} or behavioural invariants~\cite{bhardwaj2026abc}. Ours constrain what an \emph{output} may reflect and license, and we test whether agents read it correctly (Table~\ref{tab:position}). In SE for ML, hidden data dependencies are a known source of technical debt~\cite{sculley2015}, data management is a core challenge~\cite{amershi2019}, and datasheets make provenance explicit~\cite{gebru2021}. MCP~\cite{mcp2024} makes it easy to expose a predictor as a tool, but says nothing about what its output may be trusted for.

\section{A Verifier Contract for Agentic Pipelines}\label{sec:contract}
\subsection{Problem definition}
Let $c$ be an original empirical claim, $t$ a forecast cutoff, and $\mathcal{E}(c,t)$ the admissible evidence dated $\le t$. Let $Y_c\in\{0,1\}$ be the outcome of a later independent replication under a pre-declared criterion. The verifier returns $\hat p_c = \hat P(Y_c=1\mid c,\mathcal{E}(c,t))$ with an evidence record. We evaluate discrimination (AUROC), probabilistic accuracy with strictly proper scores (Brier, log loss)~\cite{gneiting2007}, calibration~\cite{guo2017}, and selective accuracy~\cite{elyaniv2010}. Selective accuracy matters because an agent may act only on its confident outputs.

\subsection{Requirements of the epistemic contract}
\textbf{R1 Temporal validity.} No feature or evidence of the queried claim may encode information dated after $t$, in particular replication results, replication-paper metadata, or post-cutoff citations. (The model is necessarily trained on \emph{other} claims' later outcomes, and the reported base rate is itself post-$t$; we treat this residual exposure as a limitation.) Calls whose $t$ falls after the outcome may be public are refused.
\textbf{R2 Label validity.} The label is the declared criterion, reconciled to published counts; alternative criteria are reported as sensitivity analyses.
\textbf{R3 Honest generalisation.} Claims from the same paper are never split across training and test data, and a claim is never scored by a model that saw any label from its own paper.
\textbf{R4 Decision-relevant output.} The output must beat the base rate on proper scores and must expose the base rate. If it is used for gating, its confident outputs must be more accurate than its unconfident ones.
\textbf{R5 Auditability.} Every prediction must be traceable to a source archive (with checksum), an evidence access date, the feature tiers used, and a model configuration.
R1 and R3 constrain what the output may \emph{reflect}, R4 what it \emph{licenses}, and R2 and R5 make both checkable. R1, R2, R3 and R5 are integrity requirements that can be enforced. R4 is different. It is an empirical acceptance criterion, and a verifier may simply fail it. When that happens, the response must \emph{expose} the failure through its base rate, skill and gating verdict, rather than suggest that the predictor is ready for decisions. We therefore distinguish three levels: a verifier is \emph{integrity-compliant} if it meets R1--R3 and R5, \emph{decision-qualified} if it also meets R4, and \emph{consumer-validated} if its output has been tested against the agents that consume it (R6, \S\ref{sec:discussion}). In our results, R1--R3 and R5 are met by construction and tested (\S\ref{sec:tool}). R4's first clause is met only nominally (B1 Brier skill \bssBone{}, paired CI includes zero), and its gating clause is not met by any harness verifier (\S\ref{sec:selective}).

\subsection{Feature-availability typing}
R1 is enforced by \emph{typing} every feature with the moment it becomes knowable.
\textbf{Tier A} (original publication): sample size; $p$-value and whether it is reported exactly; marginal significance ($0.01<p\le0.05$); converted effect size $|r|$; power to detect half the original effect; interaction vs.\ main effect; test-statistic and analysis family; publication year; and discipline.
\textbf{Tier T} (original publication): title, abstract and keywords.
\textbf{Tier G($t$)} (cutoff-dependent): the evidence graph of \S\ref{sec:harness}.
\textbf{Tier D} (replication launch): new vs.\ secondary data, the preregistered power target, and the \emph{planned} sample size implied by that target.
\textbf{Tier D$^{*}$} (after data collection): the \emph{achieved} replication sample size and the power computed from it.
A forecast ``at publication'' may use A, T and G($t$); a forecast ``at launch'' may add D. D$^{*}$ is never admissible and appears only as a leakage illustration: the achieved size exceeds the planned size by a median factor of \plannedRatio{} and can depend on how data collection went. Typing also forced us to decide about fields whose timing the archive does not record. SCORE's journal-level TOP Factor scores look like publication metadata, but their rating date is not stored. Since R1 cannot be verified for them, we exclude them.

Table~\ref{tab:req} maps each requirement to its enforcement point: the label gate (R2), the evidence cutoff (R1) and the tool boundary (R1, R3--R5). The implementation is \locPython{} lines of Python and runs end to end from public sources.

\textbf{Data and E0 gate.} The harness downloads the publication-frozen SCORE analyst package~\cite{score_g5sny} and records its SHA-256. It selects version-of-record, non-COVID replications and \emph{asserts} that they reproduce the published totals of 274 claims, 164 papers and 151 successes~\cite{tyner2026}. Of the plausible selections we tried (for example, 427 reports, or 309 claims including COVID-era studies), only this one reproduces the published totals. The set spans 54 journals, publication years 2009--2018 and six fields; 128 replications used new data and 146 secondary data.

\textbf{Evidence and cutoffs.} For each original paper, resolved by DOI (164/164), the harness caches the OpenAlex~\cite{priem2022} record and every citing work: \nCiting{} in total, with authorships, venues, topics and references. Time filtering is applied only at feature construction. The \emph{strict} cutoff, 2018-12-31, is asserted to precede the earliest replication start in the archive (\earliestStart{}); \nCitingStrict{} citing works fall before it. The \emph{launch} cutoff is the day before each replication project started; it is known for \nLaunch{} claims, and the remainder use the strict cutoff. The \emph{none} cutoff admits everything available at access time (October 2026) and is a diagnostic only.

\textbf{Evidence graph G($t$).} For claim $c$, the graph contains the original paper and all citing works dated $\le t$, with \emph{cites}, \emph{shares-author} (dependency) and \emph{shares-reference} (coupling) edges. From it we derive three feature groups. \emph{Flat evidence} covers citation counts and rates and the reference count. \emph{Graph structure} covers citing-venue entropy, field diversity, the share of cross-field citers, citer--citer density and coupling. \emph{Dependency correction} covers the self-citation share, the number of independent support groups (connected components of non-self citers under shared authorship) and the number of distinct citing authors. The graph is purely bibliographic, with no semantic supports/contradicts edges, and its features are defined per paper.

\textbf{Models and protocol.} B0 predicts the training-fold prevalence. Tabular models use median imputation with missingness indicators, standardisation and one-hot encoding (rare levels pooled), followed by L2 logistic regression. The regularisation strength $C\in\{0.003,\dots,1\}$ is chosen by inner grouped 3-fold CV on log loss. A gradient-boosted tree baseline (depth 2, 150 iterations) has fixed hyperparameters. The text model is TF-IDF (1--2-grams) with tuned logistic regression. We use stratified, paper-grouped 5-fold CV repeated over 20 seeds, and report the mean and SD over repetitions. For inference we average each claim's out-of-fold predictions over seeds and draw 2{,}000 paper-clustered bootstrap resamples. Paired differences are computed on identical resamples, and their $p$-values are Holm-adjusted over the family of 11 planned comparisons; reported CIs are unadjusted, so a CI can exclude zero while $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$ is large. The \textbf{permutation null} permutes all graph and dependency features across papers within publication-year strata, \permK{} times, re-running the full CV each time. This preserves marginal distributions and the year--citation relation while breaking the link to outcomes.

\section{The Verifier as an Agent Tool}\label{sec:tool}
Offline evaluation tells us how good a verifier is, but not whether the deployed tool behaves the way it was evaluated. We therefore implemented the verifier as a library with an MCP~\cite{mcp2024} server exposing a single tool, \texttt{verify\_claim(claim\_id, as\_of, stage)}, and enforced the contract inside the call path. (1)~\emph{R1:} for every call, the graph features of \emph{all} claims are recomputed from the raw cache with evidence filtered to \texttt{as\_of}, and calls on or after the claim's replication launch are refused. (2)~\emph{R3:} the model is refitted without any claim from the queried paper (leave-paper-out), with the same nested tuning as the harness. (3)~\emph{Stage gating:} \texttt{publication} uses tiers A+G, and \texttt{launch} uses A+D. (4)~\emph{R4:} the response carries the base rate, the cross-validated AUROC and Brier skill of that configuration, and a \texttt{selective\_ok} flag that is true only if the lower 95\% bound of top-decile accuracy exceeds overall accuracy in the selective-accuracy test (\S\ref{sec:selective}); it is false for every harness configuration we evaluated. (5)~\emph{R5:} each call appends a record with inputs, outputs, archive hash and model-configuration hash to an audit log. Listing~\ref{lst:tool} shows the actual responses an MCP client received in our smoke test: one valid call and one refused call.

\textbf{Contract tests and their adequacy.} Because the ``right'' probability is unknown, we test \emph{relations} that must hold under the contract (Table~\ref{tab:contract}). A \emph{metamorphic} test injects citing works dated after \texttt{as\_of} into every paper's evidence and requires a bit-identical output, while a positive control checks that earlier works \emph{are} counted. An \emph{isolation} test flips the labels of every claim from the queried paper and requires an unchanged prediction. A \emph{gating} test randomises Tier-D values and requires an unchanged publication-stage output. Further tests cover refusals, malformed calls, the base rate and audit records. A JSON Schema that any verifier can adopt fixes the shape of the response, and schema-conformance tests check answers and refusals against it. To see whether the tests would catch real violations, we built \nMutants{} mutants, each removing one guarantee: evidence filtering, leave-paper-out, stage gating, the launch refusal or audit logging. The unmodified tool passes all \nTests{} original tests, and every mutant is killed (\nKilled{}/\nMutants{}).

\textbf{Independent mutation analysis.} Since we wrote these mutants ourselves, we also ran a standard tool, \texttt{mutmut}~2.5.1, over the verifier module (\mutTotal{} mutants; Table~\ref{tab:mutstages}). Its own verdicts missed some kills, so we re-ran every reported survivor in isolation with a freshly restored source file, and \mutMissed{} turned out to be killed. The remaining survivors pointed to real gaps, which we closed in two rounds. The first added ten tests for the exact output schema, the true branch of the gating rule, evidence and training counts, and audit hashing. The second added four: two for the temporal boundary that R1 depends on (a work dated exactly $t$ is admitted, one dated $t{+}1$ is not) and two oracles (the launch-date fallback, and loaded evidence matching the raw cache). Because these fourteen tests were written after we saw the survivors, the final score is optimistic, and we report it next to the pre-analysis \mutVoneScore{}. None of the \mutSurv{} remaining survivors threatens a contract guarantee: \mutClsModelHyperparameters{} change hyperparameters that the contract leaves open, \mutClsCliEntryPoint{} touch the command-line entry point, \mutClsExplanationDrivers{} alter the untested ``drivers'' output, \mutClsMessageOrRounding{} change wording or rounding, and \mutClsEquivalent{} are equivalent or nearly so.

\section{Results on the SCORE Testbed}\label{sec:results}
Replication forecasting is a good testbed for the contract. It comes with realised outcomes, a natural temporal boundary, and a decision that an agent would plausibly delegate. We first ask how good an integrity-compliant verifier can be (A--E), then how LLM judges behave under the same contract (F), and finally check robustness (G).

\subsection{How good is a careful verifier?}
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the main configurations. The metadata model B1, which uses only information from the original study, reaches AUROC \aucBone{}$\pm$\sdBone{} (CI \ciBone{}). (B0 scores below 0.5 only because grouped CV shifts the training prevalence away from each test fold's own.) The evidence of signal is fragile, however. When we refit the whole protocol inside 200 paper-bootstrap resamples, so that fitting and tuning variability is included, B1's interval widens to \rfBoneCi{}, with \rfBoneGt{} of replicates above 0.5. This interval is conservative, since each resample holds only about 63\% distinct papers. The probabilistic gain is small: a Brier score of \brierBone{} against \brierBzero{}, a skill of \bssBone{}, with a paired CI that includes zero. A gradient-boosted variant ranks claims about as well (\aucBgbt{}) but has a worse Brier score (\brierBgbt{}), and the TF-IDF text model is at chance (\aucBtwo{}). The individual signals match the replication literature: replicated claims had stronger original evidence (mean $-\log_{10}p$ \fpRep{} vs.\ \fpNon{}), higher power (\fpowRep{} vs.\ \fpowNon{}) and fewer marginal $p$-values (\fmargRep{} vs.\ \fmargNon{}). Finally, an unregularised starter baseline did \emph{worse} than the base rate (Brier \starterBrier{} vs.\ \starterPrevBrier{}), a reminder that base-rate comparison belongs in a verifier's acceptance tests.

\subsection{H1/H2: Do evidence graphs and dependency correction help?}
Not detectably. Flat citation evidence added to metadata changes AUROC by \dBfourBone{} (unadjusted CI \dciBfourBone{}; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=\holmBfourBone{}$ after adjustment for 11 comparisons). Adding graph structure (M1 vs.\ B4: \dMoneBfour{}, CI \dciMoneBfour{}) or dependency correction (M2 vs.\ M1: \dMtwoMone{}, CI \dciMtwoMone{}) changes nothing, and M2 is indistinguishable from metadata alone (\dMtwoBone{}, CI \dciMtwoBone{}). The permutation null tells the same story. Over \permK{} within-year permutations (\permSeeds{} seeds), the real graph block reaches \permReal{} against a null mean of \permNullMean{} (range \permNullLo{}--\permNullHi{}; $p=\permP{}$). There may be a weak signal, but not enough to pay for the variance it adds: on the same seeds, B1 alone reaches \permBone{}. Graph features alone reach \aucGonly{}, and raw pre-cutoff citation counts are uninformative (\fcitesAuc{}), in line with~\cite{serragarcia2021}.

\subsection{H3: Does removing the cutoff inflate performance?}
Not detectably for these features. Admitting all evidence available in 2026 raises the mean $\log(1+\text{citations})$ from \logCitesStrict{} to \logCitesLeaky{} but does not improve discrimination (M2$_\infty$ vs.\ M2: \dLeakMtwo{}, CI \dciLeakMtwo{}; G$_\infty$ vs.\ G: \dLeakG{}, CI \dciLeakG{}). With so little signal, this test has low power. It does not justify dropping the cutoff, and it says nothing about LLM verifiers~\cite{sainz2023}.

\subsection{Launch-time information and a leak we caught}
Moving the cutoff to each replication's start date leaves performance unchanged (M2$_\ell$: \aucMtwolaunch{}). Our first ``launch-time'' model used the archive's replication sample size and power, and it looked like our best model. Typing showed that these are \emph{achieved} values, known only after data collection. With the planned design alone (Tier D), the gain disappears (D vs.\ B1: \dDplanBone{}, CI \dciDplanBone{}). Adding the achieved values back (D$^{*}$) recovers part of it (\dDachDplan{}, CI \dciDachDplan{}); the difference is not significant, but it points the way post-hoc information would. A field's \emph{name} (``replication sample size'') did not reveal when it became available; the typing step did.

\subsection{Can an agent gate on confidence?}\label{sec:selective}
A natural agent policy is to act only when the verifier is confident. When we rank claims by $|p-0.5|$, B1's most confident 10\% are correct only \selTopBone{} of the time (CI \selTopCIBone{}), compared with \selAllBone{} overall (D: \selTopDplan{} vs.\ \selAllDplan{}). M2's top decile does better (\selTopMtwo{} vs.\ \selAllMtwo{}), but its interval \selTopCIMtwo{} is far too wide to support gating, and mean predictions per quintile span a narrow range (reliability diagram in the artifact). No harness configuration meets R4's gating clause; \S\ref{sec:agent} shows what this means under a review budget.

\subsection{LLM-as-judge verifiers}\label{sec:llm}
An agent could also put the question to an LLM, so we evaluated four LLMs from two vendors under the same protocol. Each vendor contributed one model whose training data end before the SCORE results paper (April 2026~\cite{tyner2026}) and one after it (vendor-stated cutoffs): \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17} (August 2025) and \texttt{gpt-6.1-sol} (April 2026), \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001} (training data to July 2025) and \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} (June 2026). Each model received a system prompt dated at the strict cutoff that asked, in JSON, for the probability that a high-powered replication would succeed. We used one sample per claim and each model's lowest available reasoning setting. There were three conditions:
(i)~\emph{identified}: title, citation, abstract and the Tier-A statistics;
(ii)~\emph{de-identified}: field and statistics only, which removes the cues needed to recognise the paper;
(iii)~a \emph{memorization probe} asking directly whether the paper was replicated in SCORE and with what outcome, also asked about 100 \emph{decoys}: journal-matched papers that SCORE sampled but never replicated or reproduced.
All responses parsed, and the judging calls cost \$\llmCostTotal{}.

\emph{Discrimination and probabilistic accuracy} (Table~\ref{tab:llm}). The two post-cutoff models had the highest AUROCs of any verifier in this study (GPT-6.1 sol \llmSolIdAuc{}, CI \llmSolIdCi{}; Claude Opus 5.5 \llmOpusIdAuc{}, CI \llmOpusIdCi{}), without a statistically established advantage over B1 (\llmSolIdVsBone{} and \llmOpusIdVsBone{}). The pre-cutoff models were below B1 (\llmMiniIdAuc{} and \llmHaikuIdAuc{}). The quality of the raw probabilities varies sharply. Opus 5.5's raw Brier score is as low as B1's (\llmOpusIdBrier{} vs.\ \llmBoneBrier{}; Brier mixes ranking and calibration, so this does not establish equal calibration), whereas the other three are worse than the base rate (\llmSolIdBrier{}, \llmMiniIdBrier{}, \llmHaikuIdBrier{}; B0 \llmBzeroBrier{}). We applied the same in-fold Platt scaling, a standard post-hoc calibrator~\cite{guo2017,abbasli2026claimcal}, to every judge. This brings GPT-6.1 sol to \rcSolPlattBrier{} and Opus 5.5 to \rcOpusPlattBrier{} (Brier skill \rcSolPlattBss{} and \rcOpusPlattBss{}), the lowest of any verifier, while leaving AUROC almost unchanged. Neither recalibrated judge is distinguishable from B1 ($\Delta$Brier CI \rcSolPlattVsBoneCi{} and \rcOpusPlattVsBoneCi{}). The pre-cutoff judges improve only marginally (\rcHaikuPlattBrier{} and \rcMiniPlattBrier{} vs.\ B0 \llmBzeroBrier{}). Since a monotone recalibration improves their Brier scores without changing their ranking, the post-cutoff judges mainly fail on calibration, though recalibration needs labelled outcomes, so the judge is no longer zero-shot.

\emph{Memorization and fabrication.} Removing identifying information lowers the AUROC point estimate for both post-cutoff models (GPT-6.1 sol \llmSolIdDeid{}, CI \llmSolIdDeidCi{}; Opus 5.5 \llmOpusIdDeid{}, CI \llmOpusIdDeidCi{}; outside the Holm family). This is suggestive rather than conclusive. GPT-5.4 mini shows a similar drop (\llmMiniIdDeid{}), and de-identification also removes the abstract, which is legitimate information. Both post-cutoff models rarely recall outcomes explicitly: they named outcomes for only \llmSolNamed{} and \llmOpusNamed{} of 274 claims (GPT-6.1 sol's were all correct). Their gain from identification may therefore come from implicit memorization or from a legitimate reading of the abstract, and the two cannot be told apart from outside the model. R1 cannot be certified for these judges~\cite{paleka2026pitfalls}. The probe also exposes fabrication. GPT-5.4 mini claims to know of a replication for \llmMiniAwareReal{} of real papers and \llmMiniAwareDecoy{} of decoys, and it names outcomes for \llmMiniNamedDecoy{} decoys that were never replicated. GPT-6.1 sol does so for \llmSolNamedDecoy{}, and the Claude models for none. Unlike instance-level contamination tests~\cite{golchin2024}, the decoys calibrate claimed recall against papers for which any claimed outcome must be false.

\emph{Confidence.} Opus 5.5 is the only verifier whose top-decile gain has a CI excluding zero: \llmOpusIdTop{} (CI \llmOpusIdTopCi{}) vs.\ \llmOpusIdAll{}, gain CI \llmOpusIdGainCi{}. The result is nominal, since it is unadjusted across at least 11 selective comparisons, and it was obtained on identified input, where memorization would act (de-identified: \llmOpusDeidTop{}). For GPT-6.1 sol the gain CI (\llmSolIdGainCi{}) touches zero. The best candidate for gating is thus a judge whose temporal validity cannot be certified.

\section{Agent in the Loop}\label{sec:agent}
Contract tests show that the tool \emph{reports} its limits, not that an agent \emph{uses} them~\cite{hong2026agentactionbench}. To find out, we ran a minimal single-turn triage workflow. An LLM agent receives a claim and the output of \texttt{verify\_claim}, and must \emph{accept} the claim (the pipeline builds on it), \emph{reject} it, or \emph{escalate} it to a human reviewer with limited capacity. The system prompt is identical across workflows; only the tool output differs. The \emph{unprotected} agent sees \texttt{p\_replicate} alone; the \emph{contract-aware} agent sees the full response.
In scenario~A, a valid query at the strict cutoff, both agents receive the leave-paper-out prediction for each of the 274 claims. In scenario~B, the query is dated 2023-06-01, after the replication launched. Here the unprotected tool silently returns the no-cutoff prediction, while the contract-aware tool refuses; this covers 259 claims. Scenario~B is deliberately asymmetric. It measures what an agent does when a contract violation is \emph{silent} rather than \emph{surfaced}, not the harm of leakage itself, since the leaky prediction is no more accurate (\S\ref{sec:results}-C). All four LLMs served as agents (\agCalls{} decisions, plus \agCallsVtwo{} in the control, robustness and revised-wording runs below; \$\agCost{} in total).

Table~\ref{tab:agent} shows the results. No verifier probability was extreme (range \agPmin{}--\agPmax{}), so the unprotected agents already escalated many claims. The decisions they did make on their own, however, were close to chance, and most of the errors were rejections of claims that in fact replicated.

For three of the four agents, the contract turned these errors into escalations. Wrong automatic decisions changed by \agMiniADAutoerror{}, \agSolADAutoerror{} and \agOpusADAutoerror{} percentage points in scenario~A (GPT-5.4 mini, GPT-6.1 sol and Opus 5.5), and the same agents escalated every post-launch refusal in scenario~B.

\emph{Control arms} (Table~\ref{tab:controls}, scenario~A). Without any tool, agents mostly escalate of their own accord (GPT-5.4 mini \agMiniANotoolEsc{}, GPT-6.1 sol \agSolANotoolEsc{}, Haiku 4.5 \agHaikuANotoolEsc{}). It is the bare probability that tempts them into automatic decisions: compared with no tool, wrong decisions rose by \agMiniAUnpVsNo{}, \agSolAUnpVsNo{} and \agHaikuAUnpVsNo{} points (CIs \agMiniAUnpVsNoCi{}, \agSolAUnpVsNoCi{}, \agHaikuAUnpVsNoCi{}). A generic ``this estimate is uncertain'' note undoes part of the harm. For three agents the contract does more than that note, changing wrong decisions relative to the placebo by \agMiniAConVsPla{}, \agSolAConVsPla{} and \agOpusAConVsPla{} points (CIs \agMiniAConVsPlaCi{}, \agSolAConVsPlaCi{}, \agOpusAConVsPlaCi{}; unadjusted). Discouraging text alone does not explain the effect. Still, the contract beats \emph{no tool} only for Opus 5.5 (\agOpusAConVsNo{}, CI \agOpusAConVsNoCi{}), whose no-tool arm accepted some claims, and an always-escalate policy does just as well. For a verifier this close to chance, the contract's value is to take away the false authority of a bare number, not to improve automation, and fewer errors are not the same as better decisions: if one escalation costs $c$ relative to one wrong automatic decision, the contract-aware agent beats the unprotected one only when $c$ is below \agMiniABreakeven{}, \agSolABreakeven{} and \agOpusABreakeven{} for these three agents, and never for Haiku~4.5.

\emph{Prompt robustness.} With an independently worded system prompt (three agents, scenario~A), the ordering \emph{bare number} $>$ \emph{placebo} $>$ \emph{contract} in wrong automatic decisions holds for both GPT agents. For GPT-5.4 mini the rates are \agMiniAUPtwoErr{}, \agMiniAPlaPtwoErr{} and \agMiniACPtwoErr{}. Haiku 4.5's misuse persists (it rejects \agHaikuACPtwoRej{} of claims, \agHaikuACPtwoErr{} wrongly), and its placebo does not help (\agHaikuAPlaPtwoErr{} vs.\ \agHaikuAUPtwoErr{}). Magnitudes depend on the prompt: GPT-6.1 sol's bare-number error rate falls from \agSolAUAutoerror{} to \agSolAUPtwoErr{}.

\input{tables/controls.tex}

The fourth agent, Claude Haiku 4.5, \emph{misused} the contract. Given the full response in scenario~A, it rejected \agHaikuACRej{} of claims, and its wrong automatic decisions rose from \agHaikuAUAutoerror{} to \agHaikuACAutoerror{}. Its stated reasons reveal two misreadings. It took the verifier's weak skill (``AUROC 0.62'') and the advice ``do not gate actions on this score'' as evidence \emph{against the claim} rather than against the tool, and it read \texttt{selective\_ok} as a warning about ``selective reporting''.
It also rejected \agHaikuBCRej{} of the refusals in scenario~B, as if a refusal were evidence against the claim. We then wrote an exploratory revision (v2), designed \emph{after} seeing this failure. It renamed the field \texttt{confidence\_gating\_supported}, made the advice action-oriented (``\ldots escalate if a decision is needed''), and stated that a refusal carries no information about the claim. Haiku 4.5 then escalated \agHaikuAVtwoEsc{} of claims in both scenarios. (Listing~\ref{lst:tool} shows the original wording, which all main experiments used.) A correct tool, in other words, is not enough. Contract fields and advice are an interface in their own right, and they must be tested against the agents that read them.

\input{tables/r6.tex}

\emph{Pre-specified validation of R6} (Table~\ref{tab:r6}). Because v2 was designed after the failure and tested on one agent, we ran a fairer test. Before making any call, we froze two new wordings, the experimental conditions, a set of comprehension questions and a success criterion, and committed them to the public repository. Wording v3 separates statements about the claim from statements about the tool and lists the permitted actions. Wording v4 keeps the original fields, including \texttt{selective\_ok}, and adds a single interpretation sentence. All four agents saw v2--v4 under the main prompt, and the three agents from the robustness run also saw them under prompt~2 (\rsCalls{} calls, \$\rsCost{}). In all \rsCells{} agent--wording--prompt cells, the agents escalated every claim and made \rsMaxWrong{} wrong automatic decisions, which meets the pre-specified criterion. Haiku~4.5's misuse disappeared under both new wordings (v3 vs.\ original: \rsHaikuDvOne{} points, CI \rsHaikuDvOneCi{}), and the three agents tested on refusals escalated all of them. Asked directly about the \emph{original} output, every agent answered that the score cannot justify accepting or rejecting the claim, that the licensed action is escalation, and that a refusal carries no information about the claim (all answers correct). So Haiku~4.5 understood the very output it misused; its only comprehension error was reading \texttt{selective\_ok} as selective reporting (\cqHaikuSelRep{} of answers; v4: \cqHaikuSelRepVfour{}). The failure lay between understanding and acting. This is the knowing--doing gap described for LLM agents~\cite{schmied2025greedy,deb2026stocktake}, and it resembles agents that notice a conflict yet still adopt a corrupted tool return~\cite{yang2026overreliance}. Stating the permitted action closes it.

\emph{Not just abstention.} An always-escalate policy also escalates everything, so these results alone could mean the contract simply teaches agents to abstain. To test this, we gave the same agents a synthetic \emph{decision-ready} verifier, calibrated by construction (AUROC \rsAuroc{}). Its scores outside $[0.15, 0.85]$ cover \rsTailShare{} of claims and are \rsTailAcc{} accurate, and its v3 output permits accepting or rejecting those claims and asks for escalation on the rest. With the contract, every agent automated all of the licensed claims and at most \rsMiniVOut{} of the others, agreeing with the licence on 98--100\% of claims and making \rsMiniVWrong{} wrong decisions. With the bare number, agents also automated 64--78\% of the unlicensed claims and made 12.0--15.3\% wrong decisions. Asked whether the score alone justifies a decision, agents told licensed and unlicensed claims apart (\cqReadyQtwo{} correct, scored per claim for all agents: `yes' for the \cqReadyNlic{} licensed claims of \cqReadyN{}, `no' otherwise). The frozen key wrongly expected `yes' for all \cqReadyN{} and gives \cqReadyQtwoFrozen{}; no behavioural result uses either key, and both are in the artifact. The contract therefore tells agents \emph{when} automation is warranted; it does not merely discourage it. We stress that this is a controlled interface experiment. It tests whether agents follow a licence, not whether a real verifier can earn one, and it says nothing about how well replications can be forecast.

\emph{Decision value under a review budget.} When review capacity is limited, some claims have to be decided automatically, and the sensible use of a score is to automate the most confident ones. Fig.~\ref{fig:rc} evaluates that threshold policy on the scores themselves; it is not an agent run under an enforced budget. For the real verifier, the error among automated decisions stays between 30\% and 45\% at every coverage. At escalation budgets of 25, 50 and 75\%, it decides \buWeakTwentyFive{}, \buWeakFifty{} and \buWeakSeventyFive{} of all claims wrongly, against \buStrongTwentyFive{}, \buStrongFifty{} and \buStrongSeventyFive{} for the decision-ready tool. Agents given the bare real score sit at or above that curve. No wording can move the curve, because the contract adds no information about individual claims; under a budget, its value is to say up front that the review budget, not the tool, must absorb the risk.

\section{Discussion: Implications for Agentic Systems}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Machine verifiers are weak signals, and apparently weaker than human crowds.} Our best leakage-safe verifiers reach an AUROC of about 0.63 with a Brier skill below 3\%, while human forecasters reached a median AUC of 0.76 on SCORE~\cite{mody2026} (on different evaluation sets, so the comparison is only indicative). An orchestrator should not prune hypotheses or literature on such a score, but it can use it to decide which claims to check first, shown with its base rate and evidence record. LLM judges are no drop-in replacement, since the cutoff they are told to respect cannot be enforced on their weights. They should be wrapped in the same contract: dated prompts, de-identified controls, decoy probes, base-rate reporting, audit logs and recalibration.

\textbf{Make temporal validity a type, and test it at the tool boundary.} Availability typing caught the achieved-size leak and the undated TOP Factor fields, and metamorphic tests give regression-tested assurance that the deployed tool honours the evaluated cutoff. Tool descriptions should carry an explicit \texttt{as\_of} parameter and an output schema with base rate, cutoff, gating verdict and provenance.

\textbf{Add a consumer-side clause to the contract.} The same output moved three agents towards escalation and misled the fourth. We therefore propose a sixth requirement, \textbf{R6 Consumability}: the output must state the action it permits (for example, ``escalate''), not only the actions it rules out, and the contract must be validated against the agents that will read it. R6 is a \emph{semantic} counterpart of consumer-driven contract testing~\cite{robinson2006}, which tests consumers' expectations of an interface; here we test LLM consumers' \emph{interpretation} of the output. In a different setting, reliability labels barely changed agents' reliance on corrupted tool returns~\cite{yang2026overreliance}. Here, two pre-specified R6 wordings carried over to all four agents and, for three of them, to a second prompt, and they let agents automate exactly what a decision-ready tool permitted (\S\ref{sec:agent}).

\textbf{Ship negative controls, contract tests and audits with the tool.} The permutation null bounded the graph signal, mutation analysis exposed test gaps, and a filesystem audit found two paper identifiers differing only in case (\texttt{RJb}, \texttt{rjb}) that had silently shared a cache entry. All of these belong in a verifier's continuous-integration suite.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\textbf{Data and construct.} With 274 claims in 164 clusters, AUROC differences below about 0.04 are unresolvable, so our nulls rule out large gains, not small ones. Replication success depends on the criterion, and a failed replication does not prove a claim false. SCORE's papers (2009--2018, social and behavioural science) were selected for replication in a way our cutoff does not control, and results may not carry over to other fields. OpenAlex data are imperfect, and our graph has no semantic edges, so H1 and H2 are tested only on bibliographic links.
\textbf{Models.} We used simple, auditable models. The LLM judges used one prompt and the agents two, with one sample per claim and each model's lowest reasoning setting. Cutoffs are vendor-stated, and the pre/post-cutoff split is confounded because individual SCORE reports appeared before the consolidated paper. The probe measures only what a model will \emph{say} it knows. Refitting inside bootstrap resamples widens B1's AUROC interval to \rfBoneCi{} (\S\ref{sec:results}-A).
\textbf{Tool and agent.} The agent experiment is single-turn and offline, with controls in scenario~A only. v3 and v4 were pre-specified, but the author wrote all the wordings. Because the R6 wordings say ``escalate'', their 100\% escalation partly reflects instruction following; the decision-ready control shows that agents also follow a licence to act, but that tool is synthetic. The methodology is shown on one tool class only; a second tool is future work. H1--H3 were not preregistered, and apart from the pre-specified R6 follow-up, analyses outside the Holm family are exploratory. A mutation score measures fault detection, not proof that the contract always holds.

\section{Conclusion}
Our main lesson is about method. A verifier tool for scientific agents needs an epistemic contract, tests that enforce it at the tool boundary, and evidence that agents read it as intended; in our study, each layer caught something the others missed. On SCORE, a verifier meeting the integrity requirements discriminates only modestly, and its confident predictions cannot gate agent actions. In the loop, a bare probability led most agents into errors they avoided without the tool. A contract that states the permitted action removed those errors for all four agents and, when the tool was good enough, licensed automation that the agents followed. Replication forecasts should inform scientific agents, not gate them.
